TexasClassify the section before you search the county

In the Texas Permian, the first title question is how the land left the State. The answer decides whether the State still owns the minerals, holds a free royalty, or holds nothing. Pull the abstract from the GLO Land Grant Database (search by county and abstract number; most files are scanned and free). The class and sale papers tell you which row you're in. The survey name alone won't tell you: alternate railroad sections were often sold later as school land but kept the railroad's block label.

Relinquishment Act lease checklist
Where state and university leases are checked
  • GLO: State leases are filed by Mineral File number (MF######). Find it in the GLO GIS viewer or the GLO open-data hub (active and inactive lease layer), then pull the scanned file: lease, assignments, releases and pooling approvals.
  • University Lands: the UL GIS downloads include blocks, sections, oil and gas leases and wellbores. Reconcile them against county assignments and confirm with UL leasing staff.
  • Riverbeds: the beds of navigable streams belong to the Permanent School Fund. Check the Pecos River bed tracts whenever a lateral crosses the river.

Allocation and PSA wellsUnder long-standing RRC practice, a PSA well needs a production sharing agreement signed by at least 65% of the working and royalty owners in each tract. An allocation well relies on the operator's good-faith claim. Allocation-well permits have been challenged in court, so check current RRC rules and case status before relying on them.
Rule 37 spacing exceptionsNotice goes to affected persons, including unleased mineral owners on adjacent tracts. Your title work produces that list.

CAD mineral rolls list the owner, address, interest type and the decimal the operator is paying, usually by RRC lease number. They make a free cross-check of your decimals and a lead list for missing heirs. They're a January 1 snapshot, usually at unit level, and they can omit very small interests. A missing roll entry doesn't mean no ownership.

Texas law that changes the ownership math

These are the decisions a runsheet and fraction engine have to respect. The software flags them, and the examining attorney decides them. Check pin cites and any later history before you rely on a case in an opinion.

IssueAuthorityWhat it does to your runsheet
Over-conveyance under a warrantyDuhig v. Peavy-Moore Lumber (Tex. 1940)When a warranty deed's grant and reservation can't both be satisfied, the reservation fails first. Fill the grantee's share before the grantor's. Doesn't apply to quitclaims.
Reading the whole deedLuckel v. White (Tex. 1991); Concord Oil v. Pennzoil (Tex. 1998)Harmonize every clause within the four corners. A fraction in the granting clause can give way to the rest of the deed. Flag deeds whose clauses state different fractions.
Double fractions ("1/2 of 1/8")Hysaw v. Dawkins (Tex. 2016); Van Dyke v. Navigator Group (Tex. 2023); ConocoPhillips v. Hahn (Tex. 2024)In older instruments, a double fraction using 1/8 is presumed to mean the whole estate (the "estate misconception"). So "1/2 of 1/8" is usually a floating ½. Only language in the instrument itself rebuts that. Re-check legacy runsheets that booked a fixed 1/16.
Outstanding NPRI burdenWenske v. Ealy (Tex. 2017)An existing NPRI burdens the reserved and conveyed mineral interests proportionately unless the deed clearly puts it on one side.
NPRI and poolingBrown v. Smith (Tex. 1943); Montgomery v. Rittersbacher (Tex. 1968)A pooling doesn't bind an NPRI owner who hasn't joined or ratified. List every NPRI under a unit or allocation well with its ratification status. This is a classic DOI error.
Executive rightsAltman v. Blake (1986); French v. Chevron (1995); KCM Fin. v. Bradshaw (2015); Texas Outfitters v. Nicholson (2019)Record which of the five mineral attributes each interest carries. The executive's lease binds non-executive interests, and the executive owes them utmost good faith.
ORRI survivalSunac v. Parkes (Tex. 1967)An ORRI dies with its lease unless an anti-washout clause carries it to a new lease. Record each ORRI's scope and any renewal/extension clause.
Royalty valuationHeritage v. NationsBank (1996); Burlington v. Texas Crude (2019); Devon v. Sheppard (2023)These don't change decimals. They change how the royalty is valued. Record each lease's valuation clause (at the well, gross proceeds, cost-free, add-back).
Lease terminationClifton v. Koontz (1959); Anadarko v. Thompson (2002); Endeavor v. Discovery (2018); Point Energy v. MRC Permian (2023)A lease ends automatically when paying production stops, subject to its savings clauses. Calendar retained-acreage and continuous-development deadlines. Before honoring a top lease, check the force-majeure clause and any notices, which are often unrecorded, so ask the operator.
Horizontals across tract linesBrowning Oil v. Luecke (Tex. App. 2000); Lightning Oil v. Anadarko (Tex. 2017)Allocate by a reasonable method, usually completed lateral in the tract over total lateral, unless a PSA sets the formula. The surface owner, not the mineral lessee, grants subsurface pass-through.
Notice and purchasersProp. Code §§13.001–.002; Westland Oil v. Gulf Oil (1982)You're charged with unrecorded agreements that recorded instruments refer to, such as farmouts and JOAs. Chase every one. A quitclaim grantee generally can't be a bona fide purchaser.
Strips and rights-of-wayCantley v. Gulf (1940); Angelo v. Biscamp (1969); J. Hiram Moore v. Greer (2005); Brightwell v. I-GN R.R. (1932)A conveyance of the adjoining land presumptively carries a narrow strip (strip and gore). A railroad "right of way" deed usually conveys only an easement. Pull the railroad's acquisition instrument for every right-of-way a lateral crosses.
Adverse possessionCPRC §§16.024–.028; Natural Gas Pipeline v. Pool (Tex. 2003)After severance, possessing the surface doesn't reach the minerals. Texas has no dormant mineral act. Find the severance date before you credit any limitation title.
Technical defectsCPRC §16.033A 2-year limitation period cures many formal defects, such as acknowledgment problems. Note the recording date.

Heirship and probate in Texas

  • Affidavit of heirship (Est. Code §203.001): after 5 years of record it becomes prima facie evidence. Use disinterested affiants, record it in every county where the minerals are, and calendar the 5-year date. For large interests or disputed families, require a judgment declaring heirship.
  • Muniment of title: a will probated this way makes the order a transfer instrument. Wills generally must be offered within 4 years of death. An unprobated will doesn't pass title.
  • Small estate affidavit: passes only homestead real property. Don't accept it as the source of mineral title.
  • Transfer-on-death deeds (since 2015): search for one before applying intestacy.
  • Intestacy, separate property with spouse and children: the spouse gets a life estate in ⅓ of the real property, including minerals. The children get the remainder plus the other ⅔. Both the life tenant and the remaindermen must sign leases.
  • Community property: it all goes to the surviving spouse only if all the decedent's descendants are also the spouse's. Deaths before Sep 1, 1993 follow the older rule. Always apply the law in force on the date of death.
  • Out-of-state probate: record an authenticated copy in each Texas county (Est. Code ch. 503). Until you do, the chain is broken.
  • Missing owners: a receivership (CPRC §64.091) lets a court-appointed receiver sign the lease. Title needs the whole proceeding file. ClaimItTexas shows which operator holds an owner's proceeds in suspense.

Payment timing under Nat. Res. Code §91.402: first payment within 120 days after the end of the month of first sale, then 60 days (oil) or 90 days (gas). A division order can't amend the lease. Mergers vest property without a deed (Bus. Orgs. Code §10.008), so record the certificate of merger in each county to close the chain.
