Start hereHow a title shop gets fast without getting sloppy

Title speed depends mostly on the records, then on the people, and only after that on the software. Before you staff a project, know which of your counties have their records imaged online back to the patent and which ones still need a trip to the courthouse or an abstract plant. That one question sets your schedule more than anything else.

Rule 1: Build on prior work

Prior drilling and division order title opinions, base abstracts, operator pay decks and your own old runsheets all carry a "certified through" date. Run a supplemental from that date forward. On a developed Permian section that usually saves most of the courthouse time.

Rule 2: Match the run to the decision

A mineral purchase screen, an acquisition closing and a drilling title opinion need very different amounts of work. Pick the product before anyone opens an index (see the scope tiers below).

Rule 3: One runsheet format, every image linked

Use one runsheet schema, one naming convention and one takeoff format across every landman, contractor and tool. Every row points to the recorded image. If a row has no image behind it, it hasn't been verified.

Rule 4: Machines do the clerical work, people make the calls

Automate index searching, downloads, OCR, first-pass abstracting, fraction math and flagging. Keep people on interpretation, curative and anything an attorney will sign. Landmen run title and prepare ownership reports. Attorneys render opinions.

Where the hours go on a manual run

On a typical full run, the larger share of the hours goes to finding and pulling instruments (index searches, name variants, chasing probates and court records). Abstracting and building the chain take the next share, and ownership math and reports take the rest. Automation pays first in search and pull, then in first-pass abstracting. Of those three, only the math can be fully trusted to a machine, because exact fraction arithmetic is deterministic.

Standard operating procedureThe run, step by step

This is the sequence every tract goes through, whether a contractor in Pecos runs it or a pipeline in the cloud does. The automation section later maps each step to what can be machine-run.

  1. Intake and scoping

    Capture the purpose, product tier, tracts (as written legal descriptions), depths, deadline and budget. Add any base you already have: prior opinions, pay decks, data-room files.

  2. Map the tract before searching

    Plot it on the Texas abstract grid or the New Mexico PLSS. Note every survey or section it touches, overlaps, railroad strips, highways, riverbeds and possible vacancies. Flag state (GLO / Relinquishment Act / University Lands / NMSLO) and federal (BLM) status. Pull existing wells, units and production from the RRC or OCD, since production history tells you which leases are probably held by production.

  3. Find the base

    Look in your files, the operator's, abstract plants and prior opinions. The appraisal district mineral roll shows who is paying tax on a decimal today, which makes a good lead list and cross-check. Record the base's certified-through date. Your search window starts there.

  4. Search every index, both directions

    Search grantor and grantee for every owner and every name variant over their ownership window. Cover deed and Official Public Records, oil and gas records, probate (including other counties and states), district court (divorces, partitions, tax suits, receiverships, quiet title), judgments and abstracts of judgment, federal and state tax liens, lis pendens, plats, and BLM/NMSLO/GLO files where they apply. Where the county indexes by abstract or section, also run a legal-description search to catch misindexed instruments.

  5. Pull and image every instrument

    File each one under a consistent key, such as TX-REEVES-OPR-2019-012345.pdf or TX-MIDLAND-DR-0512-0033.pdf. No image, no row.

  6. Abstract into the runsheet

    One row per instrument: type, execution date, filing date, recording reference, grantor, grantee, legal as written, interest conveyed, reservations and exceptions, lease terms, and notes. AI can draft this. A person verifies it against the image.

  7. Build the chain (takeoff / flowchart)

    Tie every conveyance to a prior vesting. Flag gaps (no probate, name mismatch, lost instrument), over-conveyances (Duhig situations), wild deeds and anything conveying "all my interest in" the county.

  8. Calculate ownership by depth

    Work out mineral, royalty, NPRI (fixed or floating), executive rights, leasehold WI/NRI and ORRIs, tract by tract and depth by depth. Use exact fractions all the way through and round only at the final decimal.

  9. Review lease status

    Check the primary term, extensions, Pugh and retained-acreage clauses, depth limits, shut-in and cessation clauses, and recorded releases. Confirm HBP against RRC or OCD production for the lease or well, not against what the operator says.

  10. Write requirements and curative

    For each defect, give the recommended cure: affidavit of heirship, ratification, correction deed, release, stipulation of interest, probate or muniment, or quiet title. Assign an owner and a due date to each.

  11. QC with a second person

    Check 100% of the math and 100% of the chain links. Re-run a sample of the index searches and check name variants. Reconcile the result to the division order or pay-deck decimal. If it doesn't tie out, it doesn't ship.

  12. Deliver and load

    Deliver the ownership report, runsheet, images, map and requirements. Load owners, tracts, leases and interests into the land system. Update GIS. Log the certified-through date so the next run is a bring-down.
