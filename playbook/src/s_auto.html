AutomationThe title factory: what to automate, in what order

Fully automatic title isn't realistic, and you shouldn't buy anything sold that way. The realistic target is that each landman verifies three to five times more instruments per day. That comes from machines doing the search, the pull, the transcription, the first-pass abstract and all the math, while people accept or correct each field against the image. Reviewing a pre-extracted instrument should take a few minutes, against roughly 15–30 minutes to abstract it by hand. Those are planning estimates; measure your own.

Working starter code

The Permian title kit (private repository Broday6/permian-title-kit) implements this section in tested Python:

  • Ownership: the exact-fraction ledger and unit deck, with NPRIs charged only to the minerals they burden.
  • Records: Tesseract/Textract OCR, Texas and New Mexico legal-description parsers, a map check against RRC and BLM survey layers, index name variants, runsheet red-flag rules and county search plans.
  • Output: live spreadsheets for the runsheet and the deck.
  • AI: a Claude extraction step with quote checking, plus a one-command live smoke test.

Run ptk deck examples/project.json --xlsx deck.xlsx to see a full allocation-well deck built from a fictional takeoff.

Automate in this order (highest return first)

  1. Tract-to-grid mapping and "what's in the box"

    Normalize the legal description, drop it on the RRC survey polygons or BLM CadNSDI, and pull every well, lateral, unit, state/federal lease and production record that touches it. It uses free public data and saves an hour per tract.

  2. Index harvesting and document pull

    Use a licensed aggregator (TexasFile index export at $0.10/row; CourthouseDirect plant search). Run every name variant against both indices, then queue downloads. Stay within the vendor's terms (see guardrails).

  3. The fraction engine

    Use exact rational arithmetic, ownership replayed from the ledger by tract, depth and interest type, tract participation factors and 8-place decimals. It's deterministic, so you can trust it once it's tested. Reconcile its output against pay decks every month.

  4. OCR plus AI first-pass abstracting

    Type-specific extraction into a strict schema, with a verbatim quote and line reference for every field. People verify. This is where most of the speed-up comes from, and where most of the risk is.

  5. Name resolution and the chain-walking loop

    Each new party found becomes a new search, and the loop stops at the patent, the root-of-title date, or a prior opinion's cut-off.

  6. Rule-based red flags and curative drafting

    The rules raise the items in the red-flag table automatically. Draft affidavit of heirship packages, ratifications and stipulations from templates for a person to finish.

How AI title extraction fails

Vision models normalize. They quietly "fix" spellings and fill illegible spots with plausible words and numbers. The damaging cases are swapped fractions (1/2↔1/4, 1/8↔1/3), invented volume and page numbers, and corrected names. The controls:

  • Every field carries a verbatim quote tied to OCR line IDs, checked automatically.
  • A second OCR engine, with any disagreement on digits, fractions, names or dates sent to review.
  • A mismatch between the written fraction and the numeral ("one-half (1/4)") always raises a flag.
  • Ignore the model's self-reported confidence. Measure confidence by agreement between engines.
  • Never use AI super-resolution on a source image. It invents characters.

Review tiers

Tier A: a person always reviews

Anything that changes ownership: conveyed fractions and their basis, reservations and exceptions, parties and capacity on pre-1970 instruments, and every double-fraction, over-conveyance, word/numeral or illegible flag.

Tier B: sample review

Header fields where both engines agree and every validator passed. Spot-check 5–10% and accept the rest. Send every correction into the gold set.

Extraction prompt and schema

Send the page images plus the OCR text with stable line IDs (p3.l17: …), and ask for line IDs and a verbatim quote in every evidence entry. Run a cheap model first to classify and split instruments. Then run a prompt specific to that instrument type, because a lease and a mineral deed need different questions.

You are abstracting a recorded instrument for an oil and gas runsheet in {COUNTY} County, {STATE}.
Inputs: page images, and OCR text where every line has an ID like p2.l14.

Rules:
1. Transcribe and extract only what is on the page. If a value is not on the page, return null.
2. Copy names, fractions, legal descriptions and recording references exactly as written,
   including both the words and the numerals of every fraction. Never correct spelling.
3. If text is illegible, put [illegible] in the quote and add an "illegible" flag. Never guess digits.
4. Every extracted object needs evidence: page, the OCR line_ids, and a verbatim quote.
5. Record each fraction as factors (for "1/2 of 1/8": [1/2, 1/8]) and the basis exactly as stated
   (of the minerals, of grantor's interest, of royalty, of production). Do not decide whether a
   royalty is fixed or floating, whether Duhig applies, or who the heirs are. Only flag it.
6. Flag: written fraction ≠ numeral, double fractions, term language ("for __ years and as long
   thereafter"), "all my interest in __ County" language, a spouse who didn't join, an unclear
   capacity (trustee, executor, attorney-in-fact), interlineations, missing pages, no acknowledgment,
   marginal notations, and conveyances larger than the grantor appears to own.
7. List every reservation, exception, "save and except" and "subject to" clause separately,
   with its full verbatim text.
8. Return JSON that matches the schema exactly. No commentary.

__SCHEMA__

Data model: an ownership ledger, not a spreadsheet

  • Core tables: document (immutable image + hash), instrument, party (as written) linked to a resolved person_entity, and entity_link (aka, fka, merged into, heir of, spouse of, successor trustee) with evidence on every link.
  • Tracts: legal_description → tract (PostGIS geometry) → tract_fragment for save-and-except slices. Plus depth_interval and interest_type (MI, executive, bonus, royalty, NPRI fixed/floating, ORRI, WI, NRI).
  • The ledger, conveyance_event: one row per instrument × grantor → grantee × fragment × depth × interest. It stores fraction factors as NUMERIC numerator/denominator pairs, plus the basis.
  • Ownership on any date is a replay of the events in order. A grantor whose balance goes below zero raises an over-conveyance (Duhig) flag.
  • Leases, units and allocation wells carry their own tract participation factors and the source of each factor: PSA, unit designation or pooling order. RRC acreage forms like the P-16 are regulatory filings, not ownership.

Legal descriptions and GIS

Texas: abstract, survey, block, section

  • Parse with a deterministic grammar first: county, abstract (A-123), survey (T&P, H&TC, GC&SF, PSL, UL…), block (C-21, 33), township (T-2-S), section, aliquots and save-and-excepts. Use the LLM only as a fallback, and check its output against the survey layer.
  • Use the RRC digital map data (free county shapefiles: survey polygons, surface and bottom-hole well locations, lateral arcs). The GLO's statewide survey grid is licensed from a third party and can't be redistributed. Use the RRC version, or license Whitestar.
  • Irregular sections: "N/2 of Section 12" needs a geometric split. Mark it as approximate geometry, and treat the legal text as controlling.
  • The Texas vara is 33⅓ inches. Watch for magnetic bearings and check traverse misclosure on metes-and-bounds tracts.

New Mexico: PLSS

  • Resolve aliquots right to left: "N/2 SE/4" → {NESE, NWSE}. Normalize lots through CadNSDI PLSSIntersected and compare geometry, never strings. "W/2 of irregular Section 6" can equal "Lots 3–7, SE/4NW/4, E/2SW/4."
  • Sources: BLM CadNSDI (NM), NMSLO GIS downloads (UTM 13N, EPSG:26913), OCD GIS hub, BLM MLRS reports.
  • Check each layer's projection file for NAD27 vs NAD83.

The search loop

  1. Seed with the tract and its known owners (the CAD mineral roll in Texas, or the deal file).

  2. Expand every name: initials, nicknames (Jno., Wm., Chas.), married and maiden names, "et ux" forms, trusts and estates, corporate predecessors and successors. Use phonetic keys, because old index books group names by letter codes and misindex often.

  3. Search both directions: the grantor index from acquisition forward, including after death; the grantee index back toward the root. Limit each search to the window when that person could have owned the tract, with some slack for late recording.

  4. Filter hits by legal description, but always keep "all my interest in X County" conveyances and anything that cites an instrument already in the chain.

  5. Queue every new party for the same loop. Stop at the patent, the root-of-title date, or a prior opinion's cut-off. Fall back to legal-description indexes or the plant to catch misindexed instruments.

Guardrails

RiskRule
Scraping county portals and vendor sitesDon't. Their terms usually prohibit bots and bulk download, and getting around logins or rate limits creates contract and computer-crime exposure. Use paid accounts, licensed exports and negotiated bulk feeds, and get counsel to sign off on each source. RRC, GLO, BLM and NMSLO public data are free to download.
Practice of lawLabel outputs "Runsheet / landman ownership report: not a title opinion." Send legal-effect questions (Duhig, fixed vs floating, heirship, curative sufficiency) to attorneys. Drilling, division order and acquisition opinions stay with attorneys.
Owner personal dataRedact SSNs, birth dates and account numbers before pages go to any AI vendor. Use enterprise or zero-retention terms. Encrypt, limit access and log it.
Data licensesRead vendor terms for "no derivative datasets" and "no ML training" clauses. The GLO survey grid, the pyTRS PLSS parser and the Surya/Marker OCR weights all carry commercial-use limits.
Model driftPin model versions. Re-run the gold set on every model or prompt change. Store the prompt, model and schema version with every extracted value.

Recommended build: Python, FastAPI, Pydantic (one schema source for the LLM and for validation), PostgreSQL with PostGIS, pg_trgm and fuzzystrmatch, object storage with object lock for originals, a durable workflow queue, and a review screen that shows the page image with the field's box highlighted next to the extracted value.
